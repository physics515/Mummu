Read the following notes from a small-town historical society and answer the question at the end.

In 1887 the town of Marrow Point voted to build a lighthouse on the granite shelf north of the harbor. The first keeper, Ada Vennor, arrived the following spring with two trunks, a cat named Pilot, and a logbook that she filled with weather observations every four hours. Her entries are unusually precise: she recorded barometric pressure to a hundredth of an inch, noted the color of the sky at dawn, and counted the fishing boats that left the harbor each morning. During the winter of 1891 a storm destroyed the wooden boathouse, and Vennor spent three nights keeping the lamp lit by hand after the clockwork rotation mechanism froze. The town council later awarded her a silver watch, which is now displayed in the society's reading room.

Vennor retired in 1903 and was replaced by Thomas Heald, a former ship's carpenter. Heald rebuilt the boathouse in stone and added a small workshop where he repaired oars and lobster traps for local families, charging nothing but a share of the catch. His logbooks are shorter than Vennor's and mostly describe repairs, but a few pages record the arrival of the first motorized fishing boat in 1911, which he described as "loud enough to wake the gulls on the far rocks." Heald kept the light for twenty-two years.

In 1925 the lighthouse was electrified, and the keeper's position was reduced to a part-time caretaker role. The last caretaker, Miriam Oduya, served from 1958 until the light was automated in 1974. Oduya is remembered for starting the society itself: she collected the old logbooks from the attic of the keeper's house, transcribed them by hand, and organized the first public exhibition in the town hall in 1976. The exhibition drew visitors from three neighboring counties, and the proceeds paid for a new roof on the keeper's house.

Today the lighthouse is maintained by volunteers. The lamp still turns every night, although the original lens was moved to the museum after a crack was discovered during a 1998 inspection. Visitors can climb the tower on summer weekends, and school groups read excerpts from Vennor's logbook during an annual spring festival that ends with a lantern procession along the harbor wall.
